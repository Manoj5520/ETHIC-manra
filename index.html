ETHIC × MANRA · Strategic reference

ETHIC is not a gadget. It is a way to engineer behaviour.

ETHIC is the company. Its intended category is Behavior Engineering: find a real human problem, understand the behaviour behind it, and implement the smallest useful intervention that can change the experience for the better. That intervention might eventually be physical, digital, a service, an AI-enabled system or a hybrid.

MANRA is the intelligence system behind ETHIC. It researches, compares and qualifies opportunities so ETHIC does not commit money or engineering effort just because an idea looks attractive.

01 / The meaning

One company. One intelligence system. Different jobs.

Think of ETHIC as the company that may create and deliver an intervention. Think of MANRA as the system that helps it decide which problem deserves that effort. They work together, but they are not the same thing.

The company

ETHIC · Behavior Engineering

ETHIC is not defined by one gadget or product category. It is built around the common thread of human behaviour: what people try to do, what gets in the way, what they do to cope and what could help them behave or act differently in a useful way.

The current commercial starting point: find the best first product under a tight capital constraint. Physical everyday products are the present route being explored—not a permanent limit on what ETHIC could become.

The intelligence layer

MANRA · Strategic Intelligence & Growth

MANRA helps ETHIC investigate friction, understand the underlying force, study existing alternatives, expose the remaining gap, set evidence gates and recommend whether to proceed, narrow or stop.

MANRA does not make a product “validated” by naming it. Evidence must justify the decision. ETHIC owns the product commitment and outcome.

FIND→CHANGE→IMPLEMENT
Find

Understand first

Identify a real problem, repeated friction, habit, behavioural pattern or unmet need. Do not start by deciding which gadget to make.

Change

Know what must change

Understand what triggers the behaviour, what prevents a better result, and what intervention could improve the experience.

Implement

Build the smallest useful intervention

Test the simplest suitable form. Do not add technology if a clearer physical or behavioural design solves the problem better.

What “Identity Built Into Everything” means: the intended user outcome informs the problem chosen, the form, how it is handled, errors, maintenance and repeat use from the beginning. It does not mean putting the logo on everything. The fingerprint is the brand imprint—a visual metaphor for patterns in human behaviour, not a biometric-technology claim. “ETHIC” is the company name here; this page does not invent an expansion of its letters.
02 / The transformation

The thinking changed. The product has not been proven yet.

The original gadget phase was the entry point, not wasted work. It taught the team to think about affordability, portability, everyday use and customer expectations. The deeper shift was deciding to understand behaviour before selecting a product.

03 / MANRA method

Search for forces—not just annoyances.

A person may get used to a small problem and stop noticing it. Their workaround gives us a clue about what they are trying to achieve. It does not, by itself, prove that a new product is needed.

01 / SEE IT

Recurring friction

What is the person trying to do? What keeps getting harder, slower, uncertain or repeated?

02 / EXPLAIN IT

Underlying force

What physical, environmental, task or behaviour condition keeps causing the same outcome?

03 / FOLLOW IT

User workaround

Do they tie, clip, wedge, hold, reposition, search, reconnect, clean or repeat something to cope?

04 / TEST IT

Residual gap

After the workaround and current products are considered, what still goes wrong? Is that gap important enough to solve?

Existing solution→User workaround→Remaining gap

“Unofficial second interface”

The phrase names a discovery idea used inside ETHIC: people modify their surroundings when the first solution does not fully fit the task. The tie, clip, wedge or repositioning move is their unofficial fix.

What it does—and does not—prove

A workaround is a signal to investigate. It is not proof of market size, willingness to pay, uniqueness or patentability. First observe the task, check alternatives and test the gap. This is ETHIC's internal discovery language, not presented as a formal academic framework.

04 / Friction taxonomy

F1–F11 are problem types—not eleven products.

First describe the person's real task. Then choose the track that fits the friction you actually observe. The six technology tracks below use familiar situations as investigation prompts; they do not claim that a named product is defective.

LANE A · F1–F4Everyday micro-frictions · small, repeatable actions or failures
F1

Fit & Hold

Does it stay where it should?

What slips, detaches or moves when a force is applied?

What to observe: when the cable is pulled in a real setup, does the clip move, peel, or continue holding? Record surface, attachment, pull direction and repeats. The proposed cable-anchor candidate is tested against this question.

F2

Position & Access

What must a person keep holding or repositioning?

Illustration: repeated repositioning

Everyday example: someone pauses mid-task to hold a tool, move it out of the way, then pick it up again because there is no convenient temporary resting place.

F3

Tangle & Retrieve

What gets mixed, buried, tangled or difficult to locate?

Illustration: tangled leads or buried items

Everyday example: the user spends time separating cables or searching a bag for a small item. Check how often it happens and what the search actually costs.

F4

Repeated Effort

What extra action has become so normal that nobody questions it?

Illustration: the same small action repeats

Everyday example: re-wrapping, wiping, reconnecting or readjusting something over and over. Repetition alone is not enough; the consequence must matter.

LANE B · F5Human friction + a technological capability · a workflow-level bridge
F5

Human–Technology Fusion

Do not assume product failure

A person has a real task. Technology helps with part of it. What human work remains after the technology responds?

1 · Human task

Listen during a conversation and remember details or commitments.

2 · Technology response

NEO 1 is designed to capture conversation information and produce summaries, memory and action items.

3 · Remaining work to investigate

Does the user still have to check accuracy, find the right detail, organise the follow-up or take action?

4 · Evidence question

Which remaining step is genuinely difficult, how often, and for whom? Do not assume there is a gap without evidence.

This is a research example—not an allegation that NEO 1 fails. Source: NEO 1 product page.

LANE C · F6–F11Technology-led / macro frictions · each example is bounded to a specific task
F6 · Capture & Recall

“I recorded it. Where is it?”

Common example: a student records a lecture or someone saves a voice note, then cannot quickly find the exact detail later.

Investigate: was it captured, labelled and made searchable when needed?

F7 · Data → Action

“I got a summary. Now what?”

Common example: a meeting summary exists, but the user still has to extract the promise, assign an owner and add a due date to a task list.

Investigate: what steps remain between information and completion?

F8 · Context & Accuracy

“Can I trust this output?”

Common example: speech-to-text mishears a name, date or number. The user must replay the audio or check the source before acting.

Investigate: what needs correction, how costly is the error, and who verifies it?

F9 · Access & Reach

“The tool works—except here.”

Common example: a handheld vacuum cleans an open surface, but a narrow gap or deep car-seat crevice is hard to reach with the available nozzle.

Investigate: is the access gap frequent and important, or just an occasional edge case?

F10 · Setup & Maintenance

“It needs care before I can use it.”

Common example: wireless earbuds need charging, cleaning, pairing and sometimes resetting before they work as expected.

Investigate: which recurring upkeep step limits use, and in what setting?

F11 · Interoperability & Handoff

“Why am I copying this twice?”

Common example: a date or commitment arrives in a chat, then must be manually re-entered into a calendar and task app.

Investigate: where does the handoff break, and is manual transfer actually avoidable?

F5 is not F11. F5 begins with a human task and asks how technology changes that task—and what human work remains. F11 begins with a handoff between tools and asks why information or actions do not move between them smoothly. All examples here are prompts, not claims of observed market demand.

07 / Three meanings—keep apart

“Fusion” is used in two different ways here. They are not the same.

F5 is a friction category. Pattern 01 + Pattern 02 is a possible product-design direction. F11 is about tools handing work to each other. The word may sound similar, but each asks a different question.

F5 · Problem type

Human task + technology

Question: what difficulty does a person face, how does a technology respond, and what human work may remain?

Reference: NEO 1's conversation capture. The discovery is about a defined workflow, not an assumption that the device is defective.

F11 · Problem type

Tool + tool handoff

Question: where do devices, apps or physical/digital tools fail to exchange information or complete a handoff smoothly?

Reference: copying a commitment from a chat into a calendar and task list by hand.

Pattern 01 + Pattern 02 · Design thesis

Commercial simplicity + capability transformation

Question: could an easy-to-understand, low-complexity product also create a genuinely useful change in what the person can do?

This is the proposed ETHIC “hero-product” thesis—not F5, not F11, and not a final product concept.

PATTERN 01

Commercial simplicity

Small, clear, cost-conscious, easy to understand and feasible to prototype. A cable organiser is an example of the product class—not a validated ETHIC product.

  • Obvious job and use
  • Minimal parts and complexity
  • Potentially suitable for tight first-product capital
  • Still has to be differentiated and tested
PATTERN 02

Capability transformation

A familiar, wearable or portable form delivers a noticeably different capability. NEO 1 is a reference used to study this pattern—not an ETHIC-owned product.

  • Clear ability or outcome change
  • Added complexity must be justified by need
  • Look at the whole workflow, not just the device
  • No claim that copying this approach is the answer

09 / Exact stop point

What is true now—and what is not.

The honest current achievement is a more structured method for selecting the first intervention. The product breakthrough, demand and commercial results remain unproven.

Established in the working record

Strategic direction

ETHIC's intended identity is Behavior Engineering. MANRA supports discovery and qualification. The friction taxonomy and candidate-selection logic are documented.

Hypothesis / candidate

C1 · Cable anchoring

The strongest current candidate. It still needs direct observation, bench testing, comparison with alternatives, user evidence and P1 qualification.

Not proven

Product and business proof

User validation and paid validation are incomplete; unit economics and commercial proof are not established; patentability is not established; scale is not approved.

What comes next, in order

  1. Observe: document the cable-anchor failure in a real, repeatable context. A single example is a signal, not a universal problem.
  2. Test the mechanism: bench-test pull direction, force, surface compatibility, adhesive/attachment behaviour and repeat cycles. Record results rather than assuming the cause.
  3. Compare: inspect existing cable holders and other solutions. State exactly what they solve and what remains.
  4. Talk to users: use a five-person quick screen first; ask neutrally, record frequency and consequence, and score reach, pain and gap. For stronger validation, expand to roughly 20–30 relevant interviews per product as a planning target.
  5. Qualify: check all five P1 requirements. If it fails, narrow the problem or stop. Do not manufacture to rescue a weak hypothesis.
  6. Prototype and validate: only after the evidence gate, use the smallest test that can disprove or support the proposed intervention; then assess paid adoption and unit economics.
Quick five-person screen (screening only, not market proof): Reach—at least 3 of 5 independently describe the same friction before prompting; Pain—average 3.5/5 or evidence of repeated burden; Gap—name one specific step the best current solution still leaves to the user, supported by an observation or test. 3/3 → proceed to deeper study; 2/3 → narrow and retest; 0–1/3 → stop or rethink.
10 / Capital discipline

The first product is a wedge—not the destination.

The initial plan is to begin under tight capital discipline. The goal is not to build a giant catalogue at once; it is to select the best first intervention under today's constraints, prove value, learn and reinvest if the economics support it.

Target prototype envelope

₹11,500–₹12,500

This is a planning target for a first prototype/test, not money spent, funding secured or a production budget. The broader early planning range was about ₹11,000–₹13,000.

Reinvestment hypothesis

Learn → earn → reinvest

If Product 1 can solve a real problem and sell at viable economics, its profit, customer learning and operational knowledge can help fund Product 2. Each step must be earned; the flywheel is an ambition, not an achieved operating model.

First capital→Smallest test→Customer evidence→Sale→Profit, if any→Learning + reinvestment↻
Targets, not achievements: 20–30 relevant user interviews per product; at least 10% initial prototype-to-paid conversion; gross margin target ≥50%; repeat/referral target ≥20%; return-rate target <5%; concept validation target ≤90 days; at least one IP candidate for a major validated concept. None is being claimed as achieved. Five-person screening and later validation are separate stages.
11 / Evidence rules

Use the right word for the strength of the evidence.

Clear language is a core part of MANRA. It prevents a plausible story from turning into an unsupported product claim.

Tier 1

Observed

Someone directly saw the behaviour or a repeatable test recorded it. It is a signal—not yet proof that the gap is important or widespread.

Tier 2

Hypothesis

A reasonable explanation of why it may happen or why a design may help. It still needs testing, comparison or corroboration.

Tier 3

Verified

Supported by measurements, repeatable tests, user evidence or other reliable corroboration relevant to the specific claim.

Observed≠Proven·Qualified≠Validated·Validated≠Scale-ready
ETHIC's ethical boundary: behaviour engineering should improve the user's ability to achieve their own useful goal, with clear value and informed control. It must not depend on deception, coercion or hidden manipulation.
