02 / How the thinking changed

The important shift was the question—not a claim that a product has already been found.

The story is a change in the starting point: from choosing an object first, to understanding a repeated human difficulty before deciding what to build. This is ETHIC's working story, not a claim about a single historical founding event.

ACT 03 · BEHAVIOR ENGINEERING

“What is the smallest useful change?”

Start with behaviour and context, then choose the form. If a simple physical change solves it, do not add electronics. If technology adds a genuinely useful capability, investigate the wider workflow.

The first product must earn its way through evidence gates.
MANRA's discovery rule: “Search for forces, not just annoyances.” A visible nuisance may be the symptom. The research question is what repeatedly causes it, what people do to cope, and what is still not adequately solved.
03 / Product evolution

Tooth powder → toothpaste format → powered brushing → connected oral care.

This is a useful example of how a familiar job can be served through changing product forms and technologies. It is not proof that one specific friction caused every change, or that older formats disappeared everywhere.

Earlier format

Powder in a container

Powders were used to clean teeth. Users still had to take the powder from its container and apply it to a brush or other cleaning tool. Exact routines varied by period and person.

Historical reference: Smithsonian National Museum of American History.

→
Product-format change

Paste in a jar, then a tube

The Smithsonian records that Colgate marketed aromatic toothpaste in jars in the 1870s and began selling it in collapsible tubes by the 1890s. Tooth powder continued to be sold well into the twentieth century.

History and object context: Smithsonian collection record.

c. 1780 · Mass-produced brushWilliam Addis is credited with an early mass-produced toothbrush in England.
1938 · Nylon bristlesNylon made the modern toothbrush bristle possible at commercial scale.
1954 · Powered brush milestonePhilippe-Guy Woog's Broxodent helped establish the electric toothbrush category.
2026 · Camera + jet + appDyson CameraJet illustrates a more integrated oral-care system, with manufacturer-claimed camera-assisted targeting and connected feedback.
Important accuracy note

What can we say about the friction?

It is reasonable to ask whether handling, dispensing, portioning, storage or mess mattered to particular users. But the historical sources cited here do not establish one universal friction as the cause of the powder-to-paste transition. Those explanations must be labelled as hypotheses unless stronger historical evidence supports them.

The lesson for ETHIC is to work backward from products: what job does the format make easier, what did it change, and what still remains inconvenient? Do not rewrite history to fit a business idea.

History sources: Library of Congress: toothbrush history · Smithsonian: nylon toothbrush · Smithsonian: oral-care collection.

04 / The discovery method

Start from a task. Find the workaround. Test the remaining gap.

Friction means a recurring point where a person's intended task meets resistance: extra effort, delay, uncertainty, repeated action, failure or compromise.

01 / OBSERVE

What is the user trying to do?

Describe the user, task, setting and moment. Avoid vague statements such as “cables are annoying”.

02 / EXPLAIN

What is causing the friction?

Look at force, fit, context, access, information, timing and repeated behaviour. A symptom is not yet a cause.

03 / COMPARE

What do people use now?

Inspect products and workarounds fairly. A cheap clip, tape, cloth, app or existing habit may already be good enough.

04 / QUALIFY

What remains unsolved?

Show what the current solution still makes the user do. Choose proceed, narrow or stop based on evidence.

The central question: WHAT DOES THE CURRENT SOLUTION STILL MAKE THE USER DO?
05 / Micro-frictions

F1–F4: small, everyday problems that repeat.

These are problem types, not product names. The same product can reduce more than one type of friction; the actual track depends on what happens in the real situation.

LANE A · F1–F4SMALL TASK · VISIBLE WORKAROUND · TESTABLE CONSEQUENCE
F1Fit & Hold

“Will it stay where I put it?”

What slips, shifts or detaches under force?

A desk cable organiser can keep loose cable ends close to the edge instead of letting them drop behind a desk. It is an established product category sold by many makers, not an undiscovered solution.

Research question: in a specific setup, does the mount itself move when the cable is pulled? What surface, load direction and attachment method are involved?

Existing-solution reference: UPLIFT cable clips.

F2Position & Access

“Why do I keep moving it?”

What must the user repeatedly hold, reposition or arrange?

Example: a craftsperson, cook or technician needs to put a tool down briefly, but the workspace has no suitable resting spot. The person keeps moving it out of the way and picking it up again.

Research question: how often does it happen, and does it cause delay, damage, contamination or another measurable consequence?

F3Tangle & Retrieve

“Where did it go—and why is it tangled?”

What gets mixed together, buried or hard to locate?

Example: charging leads are stored in a shared drawer and become tangled or hard to identify. Cable clips or labelled storage can reduce that problem for a particular setup.

Research question: is the issue tangling, identification, reach, or a different job? Don’t force every cable problem into C1.

F4Repeated Effort

“A tiny extra action, repeated again and again.”

What repeated action has become normal?

The Caulking Finger is a useful reference to a specific task: smoothing and finishing a bead of caulk. A dedicated shaped tool can help standardise contact with the bead instead of relying only on a bare finger.

Research lesson: a narrowly scoped, minimalist tool can be useful. We cannot claim that nobody noticed this task or that it had no alternatives; those claims require evidence.

Product reference: The Caulking Finger listing/search.

Global scalability is not proof of a gap. Cable organisers demonstrate how a simple fix can be useful across many desks and places. That category already exists. ETHIC’s C1 hypothesis is narrower: whether one specific cable mount shifts or peels under pull, and whether the reviewed solutions leave a meaningful gap in the exact context being studied.
06 / The bridge track

F5: a human task meets a technological capability.

F5 is not just “a gadget” and it is not an assumption that a current product is defective. Start with a real human difficulty, study what the technology does, and investigate the work that may remain across the wider workflow.

F5 · Human friction + technology

NEO 1: make room to listen; investigate the rest of the workflow.

1
The human task

During a meeting, class, site visit or customer conversation, a person tries to listen while remembering details, decisions and commitments.

2
The existing technology response

NEO 1 is marketed as a wearable AI notetaker that can capture conversations and turn them into searchable memory, summaries, tasks and follow-ups through an app.

3
What MANRA would investigate—not assume

Does the user still need to check a name or number, find a specific detail, get consent, confirm the owner/date of an action, or actually complete the follow-up?

4
The possible residual gap

Only a repeated, important remaining task supported by user evidence could justify another intervention. If NEO 1 plus the current workflow already works well, there may be no worthwhile gap for ETHIC to pursue.

Manufacturer specifications and promotional claims must be tested in context. This page does not claim NEO 1 has a defect. Conversation capture also raises consent, privacy and accuracy questions that must be handled responsibly.

07 / Technology-led tracks

F6–F11: investigate the wider product or system.

These examples move from one tiny action to a larger product workflow. Two reference products make the idea concrete: a cordless vacuum used across different places, and a connected oral-care device that combines brushing, camera-assisted targeting, liquid jetting and app guidance.

Reference A · Access across spaces

Dyson V8 Absolute cordless vacuum

A portable powered cleaning system: one motor-and-battery unit, plus different tools for floors, furniture, narrow gaps and car interiors. A clear example of a core device adapted to multiple tasks.

115 AW stated suctionUp to 40 min stated runtime*Handheld conversionCrevice tool + attachments
  • Why it matters: cordless power and purpose-made attachments support a range of tasks, including awkward spaces, upholstery and car cleaning.
  • What it still asks the user to do: charge it, select/attach the right tool, empty the bin and maintain the filter. Dyson recommends monthly filter cleaning.
  • What to test: real runtime on the needed mode, reach in the particular gap, weight, dust capacity, noise, tool changes and cleaning time.
*Manufacturer specification, not an independent comparative test. Dyson states up to 40 minutes on suction mode 1 with a non-motorised tool; actual runtime varies. This product is a vacuum, not a blower. Its usefulness in homes and car interiors is documented; use in a café or large commercial vehicle would require context-specific testing.
Reference B · Guided precision

Dyson CameraJet electric toothbrush

A higher-complexity system that combines a toothbrush, a macro camera, a liquid jet, machine-learning targeting and a connected app. It illustrates capability transformation—not automatically a better or validated outcome.

100k-pixel macro camera*Live gap targeting*Liquid jet*App + coverage feedback*
  • What the company says it does: camera-assisted detection of gaps between teeth and a targeted jet of mouthrinse during brushing; the MyDyson app offers live view, guided cleaning, coverage mapping and personalised feedback.
  • Privacy detail: Dyson says the camera is active during live view/auto-jetting and that images are not recorded or stored on the device or in the cloud.
  • What to verify: targeting accuracy, clinical outcomes, usability, cleaning and maintenance, liquid leakage risk, robustness, cost, accessibility and whether app advice changes user behaviour.
*These are manufacturer-described features, not independent clinical proof. This is a valuable reminder: adding sophisticated capability does not remove reliability and service risks. Manufacturer update.

F6

Capture & Recall

Question: what is missed, captured too late or hard to retrieve?

Example: a person remembers that a useful comment was made in a meeting but cannot quickly find the exact moment. NEO 1 illustrates the recording/searchable-memory approach; it does not prove every recall problem is solved.

TEST: search time · missed details · accuracy · consent
F7

Data → Action

Question: what work remains after a summary, alert or output appears?

Example: NEO 1 can produce action items; Dyson CameraJet's app offers coverage mapping and guidance. Investigate whether the user knows the next step and actually completes it.

TEST: ownership · due date · completion · usefulness
F8

Context & Accuracy

Question: what must be checked before a system output can be trusted?

Example: an AI transcript may need a name or number verified. A camera-targeting system's detection claims also need testing across real users and settings.

TEST: error types · corrections · false targets
F9

Access & Reach

Question: what is difficult to reach, clean, carry or use in a constrained space?

Example: the Dyson V8 has a crevice tool for narrow gaps and a low-reach adapter for areas under furniture. Check the actual gap and attachment; no tool reaches everywhere.

TEST: reach · weight · attachment changes · access time

Setup & Maintenance

Question: what recurring setup or upkeep gets in the way?

Example: a cordless vacuum needs charging and filter maintenance; a camera/jet toothbrush adds charging, brush replacement, liquid and cleaning routines.

TEST: setup time · charge interval · maintenance burden

10 / Three different concepts

Do not confuse problem types with a product-design ambition.

Two tracks use ideas that sound similar, and a third “fusion” is a separate design thesis. Their questions are different.

F5 · Problem type

Human task + technology

A person has a friction; an existing technology responds. Study the whole workflow and the work the person still does. NEO 1 is one reference case, not an assumed failed product.

F11 · Problem type

Tool-to-tool handoff

Two apps, devices or physical/digital tools do not transfer information or actions smoothly. Example to investigate: a user manually copies a commitment from chat to calendar and task list.

ETHIC · Design thesis

Simplicity + capability transformation

A future product may combine the clarity and manufacturability of a simple tool with a meaningful new ability. This is not the F5 track and not the F11 interoperability problem.

PATTERN 01Commercial simplicity

Clear task, understandable use, controlled cost, few unnecessary parts.

+
PATTERN 02Capability transformation

A familiar form enables a genuinely useful new capability or outcome.

ETHIC's hero-product hypothesis: find one product that is easy to understand and viable to make, while delivering a meaningful change in what the user can do. It does not mean literally attaching a cable organiser to a wearable recorder. The final product, demand and business case are still unproven.
11 / What ETHIC is aiming for

A repeatable way to find, prove and scale useful interventions.

ETHIC does not need to be the most futuristic company. The ambition is to select problems that are real, repeated, important and poorly served—and build the smallest effective solution that can be tested under the available capital.

Working differentiation thesis

Where ETHIC intends to be different

  • Begin with observed human behaviour and the user's task, not a gadget looking for a use case.
  • Find the current workaround and precisely state the residual gap.
  • Prefer a simple solution where it is sufficient; add electronics, AI or connectivity only when the added capability earns its cost.
  • Put evidence gates before prototype spending and manufacturing commitment.
  • Design for use, errors, maintenance and the full context—not novelty alone.
Evidence boundary: these are intended principles, not a proven competitive advantage. They become differentiation only when ETHIC repeatedly executes them and customers value the result.

12 / Where work stops today

The method is clearer. The product still has to earn its place.

The current achievement is a structured way to evaluate opportunities—not proof that a product has been validated, is patentable or is ready to scale.

STEP 01

Observe C1

Record a real cable-anchor failure in context. Count instances; distinguish what is seen from what is inferred.

STEP 02

Bench-test the cause

Test surface, attachment, pull direction, force and repeated cycles. Keep results and failures measurable.

STEP 03

Compare real options

Review at least three alternatives, identify what each already does well and state one residual gap.

STEP 04

Qualify, then decide

Check unmet need, ETHIC fit, broader potential, a clear intervention and prototype feasibility before spending further.

Current exact status

C1 is leading. P1 has not cleared. P2 remains unclaimed.

User validation, paid validation, unit economics, patentability and commercial scale are not established. The next move is direct observation and testing—not prematurely manufacturing a product or calling it a breakthrough.
